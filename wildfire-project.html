Overview

The problem

Canada has experienced increasingly severe wildfire seasons driven by climate change. The 2023 season was exceptional — burning six times the historical average and doubling the previous record set in 1989. Yet most existing analyses are constrained by administrative boundaries that don't reflect ecological realities, and few focus on identifying zones that burn repeatedly over decades.

This project takes a boundary-independent spatial approach — analysing intrinsic fire patterns across the whole country from 1972 to 2023, then zooming in on the anomalous 2023 season with deeper clustering and autocorrelation methods.

Data

Dataset & processing

The primary source is the National Burned Area Composite (NBAC) dataset from Natural Resources Canada — vector shapefiles covering every recorded wildfire in Canada from 1972 to 2023, with attributes including year, location, province, and cause.

Raw polygon data was converted to Parquet format for performance, then fire polygons were transformed into centroid points to enable point-based spatial statistics. Events were grouped into five decade intervals for temporal trend analysis.

Methods

Spatial techniques used

Four complementary spatial methods were applied to extract structure from a large, initially chaotic dataset.

Kernel Density Estimation (KDE)
Generated fire intensity surfaces per decade, isolating persistent high-density zones and visualising how fire epicentres shifted over 50 years.
Ripley's G-function
Statistically confirmed that wildfire occurrences are significantly clustered rather than random — consistently across all five decades.
Global & Local Moran's I
Quantified spatial autocorrelation of burned area in 2023, identifying statistically significant hotspots, coldspots, and spatial outliers.
DBSCAN Clustering
Explicitly delineated 15 wildfire clusters in 2023 without shape assumptions, separating meaningful fire zones from isolated noise events.
Results

Key findings

  • Central Canada is the persistent epicentre — Manitoba and Ontario consistently show the highest fire density across all decades.
  • Southwest Canada is a growing risk zone — KDE reveals an expanding fire presence in BC and Alberta over the past three decades.
  • Nova Scotia emerged as a new hotspot in 2023 — previously absent from KDE epicentres, it appeared prominently in the anomalous 2023 season.
  • 2023 was categorically different — burned area exceeded six times the historical average, with fire sizes far larger than any previous year.
  • A small number of clusters account for the majority of burned area — suggesting targeted mitigation in these zones could be highly effective.

Reflection

What I learned

The biggest challenge was that the raw data looked completely chaotic — thousands of fire events scattered across a country the size of Canada with no obvious structure. The key insight was that visual inspection alone is not enough; you need statistical methods to confirm what you think you see (or don't see).

Transforming polygons into centroids was a simple decision with a large payoff — it made the entire point-pattern analysis tractable. Similarly, log-transforming the heavily skewed burned area distribution before running Moran's I was critical to getting meaningful autocorrelation results.

If I were extending this project, I'd incorporate environmental covariates (elevation, land cover, drought indices) and separate natural from human-caused fires — two dimensions that likely reveal very different spatial patterns.
