Overview

The challenge

Roughly 1.5% of Norwegian greenhouse gas emissions come from construction machines — a significant share from transporting rubble at road construction sites. The RoadAI competition, hosted by NORA in collaboration with Skanska Norge, challenged participants to find data-driven ways to reduce these emissions.

We were provided with real GPS tracking data, fuel consumption records, and drone footage from active Skanska construction sites — and tasked with identifying patterns that could lead to smarter, more efficient truck scheduling and routing.

Approach

What we built

The raw GPS data didn’t directly label load and dump sites — those fields relied on unreliable manual input. So our first challenge was to automatically detect where loading and dumping actually happened from movement patterns alone.

Automatic Site Detection
Used convex hulls around dumper GPS positions to characterise load sites by material type (Stone, Equipment, Soil), separating overlapping machine zones reliably.
DBSCAN Clustering
Applied density-based clustering to identify where trucks spent significant time — automatically detecting both load and dump sites from trip data without manual labelling.
Rubble Generation Modelling
Modelled the rate of stone generation at loading sites by analysing historical truck entry/exit times and mass carried — enabling optimal truck scheduling.
Route Optimisation
Incorporated elevation data from the Norwegian Mapping Authority to propose an optimal driving scheme between loading and dump sites to reduce fuel consumption.
Results

Key findings

  • Dumpers operate in smaller zones than trucks — load sites are identifiable as the intersection of dumper and truck activity regions.
  • Material type matters — Stone, Equipment, and Soil machines follow distinct spatial patterns; analysing them separately yields cleaner site detection.
  • DBSCAN effectively identified trip endpoints — high-density GPS regions at both ends of each trip clearly corresponded to load and dump sites.
  • Stone generation can be modelled linearly per truck per day — enabling predictive scheduling so trucks arrive only when needed.
  • Elevation-aware routing — factoring in terrain from the Norwegian Mapping Authority opens the door to fuel-optimised driving schemes.

Reflection

What I learned

This was my first experience working with real industrial sensor data — messy GPS pings, uneven time intervals, and fields that relied on manual input that turned out to be unreliable. The biggest lesson was that data quality problems are the rule, not the exception, and that the most important step is often understanding the data before writing a single model.

The jury’s feedback on the DBSCAN approach was valuable — they noted that slow driving is directly identifiable in GPS data, making density-based clustering on time-spent an imperfect proxy. That kind of critical evaluation is exactly what sharpens analytical thinking, and it reinforced the importance of validating assumptions before committing to a method.

Having this work published in Nordic Machine Intelligence was a milestone — it showed me that rigorous, well-documented analysis has a place in academic discourse even when it comes from a competition setting.
